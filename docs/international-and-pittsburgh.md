# Redline abroad and in Pittsburgh: what carries over and what the evidence supports

1. The correction loop exists abroad, but no country I checked publishes correction text like Seattle's; the best public data has dates or statuses only [IE1][AU3][CA3].
2. Rank: New Zealand, Australia (NSW certifiers first), Canada (Ontario), England, Ireland; Germany and the UAE are not recommended now.
3. New Zealand is the closest fit: a clock-stopping request for information on 64.3% of applications in 2024 data, reported by consent authority [NZ4][NZ5].
4. The Pittsburgh premise is partly right: City Council passed zoning amendments and the mayor signed them on October 29, 2026, but I found no construction forecast [PG1][PG2][PG3].
5. Pittsburgh's corrections evidence is separate from that law: the city says 59% of applications are complete at intake and stay within two standard cycles, so it is a cheap pack to test, not a priority [PG6].

## Per-place notes

### 1. New Zealand

**Approval and correction equivalent.** Building consent authorities must process applications within 20 working days. An RFI stops that clock until the applicant responds [NZ4]. MBIE tracks the hold as "RFI days" [NZ1]. This is the closest match to a US correction letter I found: a building-code review with a formal hold.

**Evidence of rework.**

- Infometrics, using 2024 MBIE data, reports 64.3% of applications with an RFI, an 11.6-day median applicant response, and 25 working days median elapsed for those applications. Processing was 6 days without an RFI and 11.4 with one [NZ4].
- For Q1 2026, MBIE reports 16,532 applications, with medians of 9 working days processing and 16 elapsed [NZ2]. The excerpt's on-time figure, about 95%, was garbled, so verify it.
- A third-party summary attributes about 66% of RFIs to missing documents, 10% to incorrect documents and 5% to architect-engineer misalignment, citing MBIE [NZ5]. I could not confirm that in an MBIE document. **If true, most RFIs are completeness problems rather than code disputes**, which changes what the agent must do.

**Note the scale.** Medians are short. The time saved per job may be smaller than in Chicago or Seattle (my inference). Auckland is the slowest at 28 working days total, per the same third party [NZ5].

**Public data.** Authority-level medians for elapsed, processing and RFI time [NZ5]. An MBIE page lists a June 2026 quarter report that I could not open [NZ1]. RFI letter text: not found.

**Code and licensing.** Acceptable Solutions and Verification Methods are Crown copyright, reuse terms not found [NZ8]. MBIE sponsors free access to over 120 standards, excluding joint AS/NZS and overseas standards [NZ9]. I saw no city amendment layer in what I read, so pack costs look low. Confirm this.

**Customer and liability.** The government announced in August 2025 a rewrite of joint and several liability and voluntary council consolidation, with a bill expected in 2026; I could not confirm introduction [NZ6]. In July 2026, National pledged engineer sign-off and a specialist authority for large commercial buildings, tied to the November election. That is a party pledge, not law [NZ7]. **Who signs may change soon.**

**Competitors.** Objective describes AI vetting that gives applicants early feedback before council assessment [NZ10]. BRANZ has a research programme on AI in consenting, seen in excerpt [NZ11]. A post-RFI response tool: not found.

**Verdict.** Most of the design carries over. Breaks: a document-completeness route needs to be first class, and there is no corpus to evaluate on. **Best first pack abroad if a design partner supplies real RFIs.** The market is small: about 66,000 applications a year if Q1 is typical (my arithmetic).

### 2. Australia

**The key structural fact: planning and building are separate steps, and the private sector does much of the building step.**

- NSW: a construction certificate generally follows development consent. The owner chooses council or a private registered certifier [AU15].
- Victoria: a registered building surveyor issues the permit, private or municipal. Some councils have left this market [AU16].

So a NSW council RFI on a development application (DA) is largely a planning matter, while National Construction Code (NCC) review happens later. **The closest Redline analogue is the certifier stage, not the DA.** That split is my inference from how these steps are described, and it needs practitioner confirmation.

**NSW.**

- The portal has one additional-information workflow, used by councils and certifiers, usable at any time before determination. It records free text, attached files and a status change [AU5]. The guide uses a complying development certificate as its example.
- Council practice varies: Inner West gives 21 days to respond [AU18]. An example council letter cites clause 36 of the 2021 Regulation, seen in excerpt [AU19]. I did not read the clause itself.
- **The state's headline measure is gross days.** It deliberately counts applicant waiting time. The average fell from 115 to 84 days by May 31, 2026. Targets are 90 days from July 2026, 80 from July 2027 and 65 from July 2028 [AU1]. A league table reports this by council [AU2].
- That makes applicant turnaround a council performance issue, so councils could become interested too (my inference).
- Don't mix measures: North Sydney's figures exclude stop-the-clock time, 125 days for 2024/25 [AU9].

**NSW data, my query.** The API returned records with no key required, licensed CC BY [AU3][AU4].

| Feed | Records | Useful fields seen |
|---|---|---|
| Development applications | 444,300 | Lodgement and determination dates, status, council, cost, dwellings |
| Construction certificates | 296,368 | Status, building class, floor area, units, builder |
| Complying development | 250,430 | Status, reject reason, building class, certifier number |

"Additional Information Requested" appeared as a status in the certificate feeds, in a non-random 400-record sample per feed. It did not appear in the DA sample [AU3]. There are no RFI dates or text fields. A daily snapshot could reconstruct RFI timing through status changes (my inference, untested). Records before July 2021 may be incomplete [AU4]. Whether RFI documents are publicly downloadable is not established; the guide says they sit in the application's documents tab [AU5].

**Victoria.** An RFI within 28 days of lodgement restarts the 60-day statutory clock from zero once the information arrives; a later RFI does not stop it (one council's description) [AU12]. The state's PPAR reporting has a data dictionary and dashboards, but the site blocked me, so **Victorian RFI rates: not found** [AU11]. The Planning Amendment (Better Decisions Made Faster) Act 2026 has staged commencement into 2027, so process rules are moving [AU13].

**South Australia.** A third-party reading of the 2024-25 indicators reports 16,948 requests, 15,371 on Performance Assessed applications, against 31,461 decisions, with a 60-business-day minimum response window [AU10]. These are different populations, so this is not a rate. I could not open the report. South Australia's own government release claims far shorter times than Victoria, so the pain may be lower, but that comparison comes from an interested party [AU10].

**Older national signal.** The Productivity Commission's 2013 work reportedly found stop-the-clock provisions misused to flatter timelines (via an industry group) [AU20].

**Code and licensing.** The NCC is free and CC BY. The referenced Australian Standards are not provided by the ABCB; a free reader room allows only online viewing for personal, non-commercial use [AU14]. NCC 2025 became available for adoption from May 1, 2026; state adoption dates were not checked [AU14]. **You can embed NCC text with attribution, but not the standards.** Reuse terms for NSW planning instruments: not researched.

**Customer and liability.**

- Certifiers must hold compliant professional indemnity insurance, face disciplinary penalties, and can be proportionally liable for up to 10 years [AU17]. A certifier **issues** the request, so the certifier is a possible customer for review tooling, while the applicant's team writes the reply.
- Licensing of town planners: not found. If planners are not licensed, the "licensed professional signs" premise does not apply to DA replies. Confirm before building.

**Competitors.** Archistar was named to the NSW AI panel in May 2024 [AU21]. Its page lists Blacktown, Cumberland, Hawkesbury and Wingecarribee [AU8]. Hawkesbury describes a free pilot that neither assesses nor submits applications [AU7]. The state's grants exceed $2.7M across 14 projects, aimed at pre-lodgement quality [AU6]. A tool for replying to RFIs: not found, though my searches were thin.

**Verdict.** Certificate workflows carry over well. DAs need a new planning-instrument layer per council. **Start with NSW certificates, because one portal and one national code cover many councils.** Move to DAs only with evidence from planners. Queensland and Western Australia: not researched; my one search found nothing usable.

### 3. Canada (Ontario, Toronto)

**Approval and correction equivalent.** Toronto reviews zoning, Building Code, and where applicable mechanical and fire. Non-compliance produces a notice that explains why [CA1]. Status definitions confirm the Examiner's Notice means deficiencies or outstanding items [CA2]. Resubmission rules are specific: one combined PDF at a time, revision bubbles, **revised pages only**, with supporting documents emailed to the examiner [CA1]. **This is nearly the US model**, with a different resubmittal scope from Seattle's full set.

**Evidence.** Measured cycles or first-pass rates: **not found**. A trade article cites a press report of a 135% rise in Quebec processing times (2017-2024), and says poor applications are a commonly blamed cause, without numbers [CA9]. Vancouver's Auditor General examined permit fees and cost recovery in 2023, not cycles [CA13]. **Design fit is strong here; proof of pain is not.**

**Public data, my query.** Toronto's Active Permits feed has 200,322 rows under the city's open licence, refreshed daily. 2,920 rows show Examiner's Notice Sent and 257 show Response Received. It has application and issue dates, and a revision number. It has no comment text or review-cycle field [CA3]. Vancouver's data was not checked.

**Code and licensing.** National model codes are free electronically through NRC [CA11]; the 2025 edition is catalogued, and reuse terms were not found. Ontario's code is O. Reg. 163/24 on e-Laws, amended by later regulations through at least October 2025 [CA12]. **So a pack needs national, provincial and municipal layers,** which the earlier report's layering already anticipates.

**Competitors.** This is the most crowded place I checked. Toronto launched a voluntary CivCheck residential pre-check on August 31, 2026 [CA4]. Markham and Kingston use or are adopting Archistar, and Burnaby runs a tool called AI PreCheck, which is Archistar's product name [CA5][CA7][CA6]. Sudbury approved an $800,000 pilot [CA8]. Hamilton and Burlington are in the same trade article [CA9]. NRC ran a challenge on automated code checking that closed in August 2026 [CA10]. All of these sit before or at intake. The post-notice response step appears open, but I did not search for it specifically.

**Verdict.** Highest process carry-over alongside New Zealand. Lacks evidence and comment text. **Worth a pack only with a partner firm in Ontario.**

### 4. England (and a note on Scotland)

**Two systems.**

- **Planning:** local authorities received 78,700 applications in April-June 2026 [UK5]. Councils describe very high invalid rates on receipt: Luton about 70%, Trafford about 60%, Tewkesbury about 45%, Oadby and Wigston over 75% in a 2024 draft [UK6]. These are self-reported and unaudited. **National invalid rate: not found.**
- **Gateway 2:** BSR is the building control authority for higher-risk buildings, and applications must be complete to be assessed [UK3].

**Gateway 2 evidence is measured, and improving.** June-August 2026, as of August 31 [UK1]:

| Category | New in | Decisions | Invalid or withdrawn | In progress | Approval rate | Median approval |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New higher-risk buildings | 63 | 50 | 5 | 147 | 92% | 22 weeks |
| External remediation | 109 | 118 | 20 | 327 | 89% | 33 weeks |

The approval rate excludes invalid and withdrawn cases [UK2]. In the May-July release, **internal works on higher-risk buildings looked worst**: 448 new, 166 decisions, 257 invalid or withdrawn, 981 in progress, 77% approval, 32-week median [UK2]. In 2025-era reports, rejection or invalidity was far higher, about 40% plus 35% in one account [UK4]. Comparing these periods is unsafe. One tracker reports 82% overall for the 12 weeks to August 1, or 287 of 349 decisions [UK8].

**What breaks.** RIBA says most rejections come from incomplete applications, with some including the wrong plan entirely [UK3]. The failure is a refused submission, not an iterative comment letter. One vendor reports early-adopter submissions averaging 8,800 pages [UK9]. **That is pre-submission document assembly, a different product from Redline.** The internal-works segment might fit better, since jobs are smaller (my inference).

**Public data.** BSR aggregates only [UK1]. Comment-level or request-level data: not found. planning.data.gov.uk fields were not verified.

**Code.** Approved Document copies I saw carry Open Government Licence wording, though not all were confirmed. Following them is not obligatory [UK10]. British Standards terms: not researched.

**Customer and liability.** Principal Designers and developers carry Gateway 2 duties, per RIBA [UK3].

**Competitors.** Truelens claims 73% less time on compliance checks; those are vendor claims [UK9]. MHCLG's Extract is free to every English planning authority as of June 2026 [UK7]. A decision-support prototype began alpha trials in May 2026 with Barnet, Camden and Dorset, with national rollout planned from 2027 if trials succeed, and an officer approving every assessment [UK12].

**Verdict.** Largely a new product, in a market where approval rates are rising. **Not a pack.** Revisit only if internal-works designers report comment-style queries.

**Scotland may fit better, but is unassessed.** Local authority verifiers issue a "first report", with a 95%-within-20-days target [UK11]. National performance is reported as steady, with no percentages in the text I read [UK13]. Edinburgh received 4,297 applications in 2025/26 [UK14]. It looks like a correction letter, but I did not confirm its contents.

### 5. Ireland

**Approval and correction equivalent.** Planning authorities issue further-information requests. Building control is different: most work is certified by an Assigned Certifier rather than reviewed by the authority, with an opt-out route, per a consultant's guide [IE5]. **So there may be no building-code correction letter for most projects** (my inference). Fire and accessibility certificates were not researched.

**This is the best public request-level dataset I found outside the US.** The national dataset has 510,984 rows with request and receipt dates for FI, CC BY, updated weekly [IE1][IE2].

| Received | Applications | With FI request | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 36,805 | 9,813 | 26.7% |
| 2021 | 44,336 | 12,730 | 28.7% |
| 2023 | 36,196 | 10,115 | 27.9% |
| 2024 | 38,018 | 10,421 | 27.4% |
| 2025 | 39,715 | 10,918 | 27.5% |

- **A published figure hides a data gap.** One analysis site reports 27.5% for 2025 [IE3]. That matches my count, but Cork City and Cork County together hold 3,838 rows in 2025 and record no FI at all. Excluding them, the share is **30.4%**. Recent applications may also gain FI later, so 2025 is likely low.
- **Spread:** in 2025, Monaghan 56.5%, Dun Laoghaire Rathdown 12.3%. Dublin City 16.5%.
- **Cost of FI:** among decided 2024 applications outside Cork (n=28,096), 33.6% had FI. Median time to decision was 148 days with FI and 51 without. Median applicant response was 62 days, n=9,307. **Complex applications attract FI, so the 97-day gap is not all caused by FI.**

**Comment text.** One record with FI that I read showed dates only, and no letter [IE4]. That is one authority's page. Other councils' file systems were not checked, and Dublin City rows have no link in the sample I saw.

**Code.** The substance would be development plans rather than a building code (my inference). The Planning and Development Act 2024 is commencing in stages, and the excerpt I saw was last updated in October 2025, so the current position is unverified [IE6].

**Competitors.** Analysis sites using this data [IE3][IE7]. AI tools from authorities or for applicants: not found.

**Verdict.** The market is small, and the content is planning rather than building. **Use it as a free test bed for measuring RFI timing, and as a template for what to ask other governments to publish.** Don't build a product pack first.

### Not recommended now

**Germany.** Wiesbaden checks completeness first, usually reports within 14 days, and can reject incomplete files [DE1]. Thuringian districts have tested AI review since autumn 2025, and one concept paper keeps the decision on requests for documents with humans [DE2]. National rates, public comments and DIN reuse terms: not found. My one search also supports only city-level process claims. Language and state-level rules would raise costs (my inference).

**UAE.** Dubai announced on August 9, 2026 a system to review villa drawings against the Dubai Building Code and issue permits automatically, on a 26-week rollout, with instant feedback [UA1]. More than 10,700 permits were issued in Q1 2026 [UA1]. It is unbuilt, and a government pursuing automatic issuance leaves less room for a reply tool (my inference). Public comment data: not found.

## Pittsburgh verdict

### Is the premise right? Partly.

**What actually passed.** Local law, from Pittsburgh City Council. Not a county or state measure.

| Item | What I found | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Pittsburgh City Council; signed by Mayor Corey O'Connor | [PG1] |
| Signing | Thursday, October 29, 2026. The city page, written earlier, schedules it for "Wednesday" October 29, which is the wrong weekday | [PG1][PG24] |
| Final vote | Tuesday, October 27, 2026, per the city's engagement page; a Post-Gazette headline of that date agrees | [PG24][PG4] |
| Vote count | **Final count not found.** The October 21 preliminary vote was unanimous | [PG2][PG3] |
| Status | Signed two days ago. Effective dates not confirmed. The sponsor told WESA the parking and ADU parts could take effect the week after final approval; fee-related parts need a trust fund bill | [PG1][PG2] |

**What it does.**

- **First bill**, Bill 2025-1545 [PG24][PG5]: a voluntary affordable housing bonus, with extra height or density in exchange for lower-priced units, at 35-year affordability; a $25-per-square-foot fee alternative; no minimum parking; wider accessory dwelling units [PG2][PG3]. The trust fund for those fees still needs separate legislation [PG2][PG3].
- **Second bill:** simplifies and modernizes zoning [PG1]. The mayor's summary names a first zoning package as Bill 2026-0834 [PG6]. Treating them as the same bill is my inference; I could not reach legislative records.
- **An earlier related law:** Bill 2025-1579 on minimum lot size was signed May 7, 2025 and is in effect [PG24].

**Where the premise goes wrong.**

- **"Urbanization law" overstates it.** These are targeted zoning amendments. The mayor himself framed them as a first step, and the full code rewrite is still ahead [PG1][PG6].
- **The strongest original provision was dropped.** The 2024 bill required 10% affordable units in projects of 20 or more units. Council replaced that with a voluntary program [PG3].
- **"A lot of construction" is unsupported.** None of the coverage I read contains a forecast [PG2][PG3][PG5]. One advocate called it "the most significant zoning reform in Pittsburgh in the last four decades" [PG5], which describes the change in rules, not the volume of building. An opponent warned about building heights [PG5].
- **Context cuts against easy demand assumptions.** An estimated 20,000 homes are vacant, over 15% of stock, alongside a shortage of about 11,000 affordable homes for the lowest-income residents [PG16].

**The closest state action is not law, as far as I found.** The House passed an ADU bill, HB 2186, 139-62 in June, and SB 1346 cleared a Senate committee 10-1 [PG17]. HB 858, which passed both chambers, concerns blight, not zoning [PG17]. Nothing later was found, so check the General Assembly's tracker.

### Corrections evidence, on its own terms

**There is real evidence, and it does not depend on the zoning law.**

- **The key number.** The city analyzed nearly 12,000 BDAs submitted since 2024. It reports that 59% are complete at intake and stay within two standard review cycles [PG6]. The count is printed as "5,4000" in the source, an obvious typo for just under 5,400. **So about 41% miss that bar.**
- **The denominator is unclear.** 5,400 at 59% implies about 9,150, close to the 9,000-plus issued permits, which include simple permits with no plan review [PG6]. If so, the plan-reviewed share may be worse (my inference).
- **Qualitative.** The March review reported repetitive review cycles, unclear requirements and unpredictable timelines [PG11][PG12].
- **City time fell fast.** Median department review time for BDAs dropped from 21 days in January to 7 in August 2026 [PG6]. Targets are 30 business days initial and 15 revision for commercial, 15 and 8 for residential [PG8]. **Time with the applicant: not published.** Average cycles: not published.
- **Audits.** Permit-delay audit by the Controller: not found. The closest was a 2011 audit of the predecessor bureau [PG22].

**Compared with other places.** This is thinner than Seattle or San Francisco, and better than anything found for Illinois suburbs, Los Angeles or Philadelphia in the earlier report.

### Fit

- **Correction form.** PLI issues a formal plan review record listing comments and corrections with **code references per item**, and applications are abandoned after 180 days without a response [PG8]. That fits the current data model well. Revisions go through the portal [PG8].
- **Portal.** OneStopPGH, on Computronix [PG10]. Since July, a tracker shows review steps and comments to applicants, and feedback is centralized [PG6][PG16]. **Bluebeam electronic review is planned for Phase II** [PG6], so resubmittal format may change soon.
- **Code.** State Uniform Construction Code. 2021 ICC base codes and 2018 accessibility codes from January 1, 2026 [PG13]. Local amendments sit in Section 1002, written against 2003 I-code sections and enforced by topic [PG13]. Transition depended on contract and application dates [PG14]. **That old-numbering quirk is a good test of edition handling.** ICC redistribution is unresolved, as in the earlier report.
- **Other-agency routing.** Allegheny County Health Department handles plumbing permits [PG10]. DOMI, City Planning and others review too [PG6].
- **Public data.** [WPRDC PLI Permits][PG9] updates daily, latest issue date October 29, 2026, with 66,559 rows. Its 19 fields include type, description, value and issue date. **It has no application date, cycle count, or comment field**, so cycles and duration cannot be computed from it.
- **Are correction comments public? Not found.** OneStopPGH Insights offers public record search [PG19], but I could not confirm that it exposes comments. Pennsylvania right-to-know requests were not explored.

### Risks

The city is working on this same problem. Phase I began work on a procurement request for information on intake and readiness tools [PG6], and the mayor said he would explore AI to flag missing information [PG11]. Phase II will analyze why projects exceed standard review and publish a dashboard [PG6]. CivCheck or Archistar adoption in Pittsburgh: not found. **The new median also suggests the department-side delay is shrinking, which may raise the share of remaining time that sits with applicants, but no data shows this yet.**

### Recommendation

**Don't pitch Pittsburgh on the zoning law.** Treat it as a modest candidate on its own evidence: one official measure, code-cited comments, and a statewide code shift. Before building, ask AIA Pittsburgh, whose committees meet PLI [PG20], for 10-20 real plan review records. The Phase II dashboard may also settle the applicant-time question for free. Estimated pack: about one week. It should not displace Seattle, which has a real evaluation corpus.

## What the architecture must change to work abroad

These add to the five changes in `docs/locale-research.md`: edition by date, layered code, structured response format, comment sets, and portals keyed by vendor. Current state: `profile.json` holds one free-text `code_basis`, one `correction_process`, and an `id` for a place; tools are `search_code` and `get_section`; routes include `licensed_review`, `agent_draft` and `client_input`.

1. **Key a pack by stage and authority type, not just place.** The same address has separate planning and building steps [AU15][UK3][IE5]. A private certifier in NSW or a surveyor in Victoria is an authority that isn't a place [AU15][AU16]. Add `stage` and `authority_type`.
2. **Model instruments, not just codes, with legal force attached.** Planning replies would rest on plans and local controls rather than a model code (my inference from the planning and building split above). Some documents are one way to comply rather than a requirement [UK10][NZ8]. `code_basis` should become `instruments[]`, with `legal_force` such as mandatory, deemed-to-satisfy or guidance. `get_section` must say which it returned. Otherwise a draft could claim the code **requires** something that is only an accepted route.
3. **Add a completeness path.** Missing documents dominate in New Zealand and at Gateway 2 [NZ5][UK3]. These have no code section to cite. Add a `required_documents` table and a `missing_document` category. `get_section` returning nothing should not read as a failure on those items.
4. **Generalize the clock.** It stops in New Zealand [NZ4], restarts from zero in Victoria if the request is early [AU12], is ignored in the NSW headline metric [AU1], has a 60-business-day floor in South Australia [AU10], and runs out at 180 days in Pittsburgh [PG8]. Add `clock_rule`, `response_deadline`, and a calendar type, since working days and statutory days differ [NZ4][AU12].
5. **Make sign-off a looked-up role.** `licensed_review` assumes a licensed professional. Abroad the role may be a planner, an Assigned Certifier, a Principal Designer, or a certifier who is also the issuer [AU17][IE5][UK3]. Rename it `professional_review` and read the role from the pack. The opening line of `agent/instructions.md`, that a licensed professional's name goes on the resubmission, should become pack-conditional. New Zealand's sign-off rules may also change [NZ6][NZ7].
6. **Add terminal states.** A refused or invalid submission is a restart, not another cycle [UK3][UK6][AU3]. Add `invalid`, `refused` and `relodged`. Cycle counts must not treat these as rounds.
7. **Track text rights per source.** NCC text can be embedded with credit [AU14]. Australian Standards cannot, under the reader-room terms [AU14]. Joint AS/NZS standards are outside New Zealand's free program [NZ9]. Add `text_license` and `reference_only`. Default to section ids and your own paraphrase, as `code.json` does.
8. **Allow packs with no comment corpus, and label them.** No country yielded a Seattle-style file. `comments.jsonl` must stay optional, and a pack should carry `evaluation_status`, for example "partner letters, n=25" or "unevaluated". Status feeds [AU3][CA3] and date fields [IE1] can validate timing claims, **but not draft quality**. Results from Seattle do not transfer to these places.
9. **Key adapters at state or country level where a single system exists.** NSW has one portal [AU5]. Objective runs across New Zealand councils, though its reach is unknown [NZ10]. Toronto takes revised pages only [CA1], so `resubmittal_scope` must allow that.
10. **Localize.** Terms (RFI, FI, additional information, Examiner's Notice), spelling, and metric units. Data residency may come up: one UK vendor promotes UK/EU storage [UK9]. Privacy law in these countries was not researched.

## Recommendations

1. **Get real letters before any pack abroad.** Ask for 20-30 RFIs each from New Zealand and NSW firms. Without them you cannot measure anything.
2. **New Zealand first, then NSW certificates,** if a partner appears. Canada only with a partner.
3. **Sample before concluding text is unavailable.** Check about 50 NSW DA trackers and 10 Irish council file systems for public RFI letters. My checks here were too few to rule it out.
4. **Use Ireland's dataset now** for a credible timing analysis, with the Cork correction.
5. **Pittsburgh:** one AIA-Pittsburgh conversation and one records request, then decide.

## Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether non-US RFIs are code-comment shaped at all.** If New Zealand's missing-document share is right and holds elsewhere, a citation-focused agent is less valuable than a completeness checker.
- **Comment text availability.** "Not found" reflects limited searching, not confirmed absence. Records requests were not explored.
- **Blocked primaries.** MBIE, PlanSA and Victorian PPAR numbers need checking in the originals.
- **Willingness to pay and market size.** Not measured anywhere here. Volumes are not revenue.
- **Pittsburgh specifics.** The final vote count, effective dates and the second bill's number are unconfirmed, and it is too early for any construction effect.
- **Fast-moving rules.** New Zealand's election and liability bill, Victoria's staged Act, and Pittsburgh's Phase II could each change the design inputs within a year.
